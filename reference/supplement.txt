                                         I.   INTRODUCTION


  Symmetries of crystal magnetic orders in magnets with weak spin–orbit coupling (SOC) are described by
spin space groups (SSGs), in the same way that symmetries of nonmagnetic crystals are described by space
groups. In recent years, SSGs have been enumerated mathematically for all parent space groups. Unlike the
case of space groups and crystal orders, however, the correspondence between SSGs and magnetic orders
has not been established. Given an SSG, it is not clear which magnetic order it describes; conversely, given
a magnetic order, one usually has to search a large catalogue of SSGs to identify its symmetry group. This
gap between mathematical objects and physical reality confines the full power of the symmetry method
and hinders the rapid development of unconventional magnetism.
                                                                                                             3

   To appreciate this deficiency, it is instructive to compare SSGs with space groups. Space groups are
organized in a hierarchical classification that is both mathematically rigorous and physically meaningful:
crystal families, Bravais lattices, geometric classes, and arithmetic classes. Each level of the classification
corresponds to universal geometric properties of crystal orders, and the hierarchy provides a framework
in which crystals can be organized and understood by symmetry. Existing classifications of SSGs, by
contrast, do not encode enough physical information. At the coarsest level, SSGs are classified by their
parent space groups and by the dimensions of their magnetic orders, which does provide basic physical
information. At the next level, however, the classifications are mainly algorithmic rather than physical.
In Liu’s enumeration framework, SSGs are classified into t-type, k-type and g-type according to which
subgroups of the parent group are retained: t-type keeps the translational subgroup and reduces the point
group, k-type keeps the point group and reduces the translational subgroup, and g-type reduces both.
This classification is too coarse to provide enough physical insight. In Song’s enumeration framework,
SSGs are classified into 16 types according to the irreducible-representation content of the homomorphism
from the parent space group to O(3). This classification is more intricate, but it is unclear how these SSG
types correspond to magnetic orders.
   The lack of physical meaning in classifications based on existing enumeration algorithms does not mean
that algorithms must be at odds with physical meaning; it only means that improper algorithms are. The
algorithms of Liu, Fang and Song do not adequately exploit the group structure of SSGs and therefore
rely on brute-force enumeration. The central task to enumerate SSGs is to enumerate inequivalent ho-
momorphism ϱ : G → O(3), where G is a space group. The algorithm adopted by Liu and Fang first
enumerate normal subgroups of a given parent space group G, then for each subgroup L, take it as the
kernel of ϱ, i.e., associate L with trivial spin operation. Finally, enumerate injective homomorphism from
G/L to O(3), which gives nontrivial spin operations. This method needs to enumerate numerous normal
subgroups. Since the translation subgroup T ⊂ G is an infinite group, there are infinite normal subgroups
of G. For practicle calculation, one has to use a cutoff index ik of T. In Liu’s work, ik = 18, while in
Fang’s work, ik = 12. The cutoff makes the enumeration incomplete. Also, this method is incapable
to include incommensable magnetic orders. In Song’s framework, Hom(G, O(3)) is enumerated by com-
bining irreducible representations of G into O(3) representation of G. This method is complete since it
can deal with continuous family of SSGs and thus include incommensable magentic orders. However, it
also requires massive combination of irreducbile representations of space groups and the physical meaning
is unrevealed. As a comparison, the Zassenhaus algorithm enumerate all space groups, and it naturally
provides the classification framework of space groups and crystal orders. The success of it stems from the
fact that it exploits the group structure of space groups. In general, a space group is an extension of Z3
by a finite group P ⊂ O(3),

                                           1 → Z3 → G → P → 1.                                             (1)

The embedding of Z3 in R3 defines the lattice group T. The lattice system is determined by the normalizer
N (T) of T in E 3 = R3 ⋊ O(3), and the Bravais type is further determined by the action of N (T) on T.
The point group P is a subgroup of N (T), which determines the geometry class (Q-class in Zassenhaus
algorithm), then the action of P on T determines the arithmetic class (Z-class in Zassenhaus algorithm).
  Inspired by above method, we can also treat translations and point group elements seperately in enu-
meration of SSGs. This leads to a new enumeration algorithm that is more efficient and physical. To
enumerate all ϱ : G → O(3), we first consider the restriction of ϱ to the translational subgroup, i.e., the
homomorphism ϱT : T → O(3), which we call the translation holonomy. ϱ can be decomposed into its
                                                                                                           4

SO(3) part ρ : G → SO(3) and an antiuintary Z2 grading χ, i.e., ϱ = χρ. The image ρT (T) of the transla-
tional subgroup in SO(3) admits only three abelian classes: the trivial group, SO(2) and Klein four group
V4 . Thus we can classifies SSGs into three basic translation types according to ρT (T). For each type of
ρT (T), the spin operations of point group elements should be in normalizer of ρT (T) in SO(3), which con-
strains the possibilities. For ρT (T) = SO(2) or V4 , ρ can be characterized by propogation wave vectors.
The covariant condition between translations and point-group elements leads to physical constraint on
propogation wave vectors. The enumeration can finally reduced to enumerating possible wave vectors and
1-cocycles of the point-group. At each level of the enumeration, the mathematical data carries concrete
physical information, providing a physical classification framework for SSGs, which makes numerous SSGs
understandable.
   As a basic step, we classifies SSGs according to the translation holonomy ϱ. Based on the three basic
translation types, we further classifies SSGs into 17 translation types according to ϱT (T), we call the 17
types as spin lattice types. Each spin lattice type corresponds to a type of magnetic order and exhibits
unique Bragg peaks and polarizations in neutron diffraction experiments. Thus, our framework bridges
mathematical SSGs and magnetic orders in a transparent way, and makes SSG a potentially workable
symmetry framework to analyze experiment datas. We discuss how existing magnetic orders fit into our
framework, and briefly discuss the relationship between Bertaut theory and our framework.
   Our classification framework immediately leads to a number of universal physical consequences, because
it supplies the proper concepts for stating universal properties of SSGs and magnetic orders, which, are
usually hidden in previous frameworks. We give four examples: exact magnetization compensation enforced
by certain spin-lattice types, an exact group-theoretic criterion for noncommuting Brillouin zones, a new
type of 3Q magnetic order with vanishing octupoles, and the symmetry protection of strictly collinear
momentum-space spin textures in coplanar magnets.
  We also discuss the relationship between our framework and previous frameworks, especially the frame-
work of Song. There is a systematical way to map SSGs in Song’s framework to our classification framework.
With this, we reveals some hidden pattens in Song’s table of SSGs.
   This Supplemental Material presents the complete derivations behind the results presented in the Letter.
Section II develops the framework of antiunitary grading and translation holonomy and applies it to the
enumeration of noncoplanar SSGs. Section III extends the enumeration to coplanar and collinear SSGs,
and Sec. IV organizes the classification into a hierarchy of SSG classes, from translation holonomy types
and spin Bravais lattice types up to spin arithmetic crystal classes and spin crystal classes. Section V then
discusses the 17 universal spin-lattice types and the magnetic orders they describe, and Sec. VI derives
their neutron-diffraction signatures and their connection to Bertaut’s representation analysis. Section VII
derives the universal physical consequences, Sec. VIII relates our classification to previous enumeration
frameworks, and Sec. IX concludes.

cases of noncoplanar SSGs and are treated in Sec. III.

                                             A.   Antiunitary grading


  A noncoplanar spin configuration possesses no nontrivial spin-only operation. A general element of a
noncoplanar SSG G therefore takes the form {ϱ(g)|g}, where g acts on the lattice and ϱ(g) ∈ O(3) acts on
the magnetic moment field. Since the spin-only operations are trivial, G ∼
                                                                         = G, where G is the parent space
group; enumerating all inequivalent noncoplanar SSGs for a given G is thus equivalent to classifying all
inequivalent homomorphisms ϱ : G → O(3).
  Because O(3) ∼ = SO(3)×ZT2 , where ZT2 = {E, P} is generated by the spin inversion P = diag(−1, −1, −1)
and is realized by the time-reversal operation T , every ϱ factorizes as

                             ϱ(g) = χ(g)ρ(g),        χ : G → ZT2 ,       ρ : G → SO(3).                              (2)

Here χ is a Z2 grading that specifies the unitary or antiunitary character of g, while ρ is an ordinary
homomorphism. Correspondingly, the set of homomorphisms factorizes as

                               Hom G, O(3) ≃ Hom G, SO(3) × Hom G, ZT2 ,
                                  (︁      )︁    (︁       )︁    (︁     )︁
                                                                                                                     (3)

so that the grading and the SO(3) part can be enumerated separately and then combined. The inequivalent
gradings χ are classified by H 1 (G, Z2 ) and correspond to the type-I, III and IV magnetic space groups with
Opechowski-Guccione (OG) parent space group G.1 But here we note, the mapping between H 1 (G, Z2 ) and
type-I, III, IV magnetic groups is multi-to-one, since classification of magnetic groups considers additional
equivalence relations. We do not consider these additional equivalence relations at the stage of enumeration
of Z2 grading, untill the whole Hom(G, O(3)) is obtained.
   The enumeration of Z2 gradings of G can be done by enumerating all inequivalent normal subgroups of
G with order |G|/2. Then the task reduces to the enumeration of Hom(G, SO(3)).


                     B.   Translation holonomy and the three basic translation types


  The space group G is an extension of the point group P by the abelian translation subgroup T,

                                               1 → T → G → P → 1.                                                    (4)

Restricting ϱ to T defines the translation holonomy ϱT ≡ ϱ|T : T → O(3), with its SO(3) part denoted by
ρT ≡ ρ|T ; the restriction of the grading is χT ≡ χ|T , so that ϱT = χT ρT . Because T is abelian, its image
ρT (T) must be an abelian subgroup of SO(3). Up to global SO(3) conjugation there are exactly three
classes of abelian subgroups of SO(3):
   Type 0 (Trivial): ρT (T) = {E}.
   Type 1 (Uniaxial): subgroups of SO(2)n , generated by rotations about a fixed axis n.
   Type 2 (Biaxial): the Klein four-group V4 = {E, Rx (π), Ry (π), Rz (π)}.
   These three classes have qualitatively different structures and classify SSGs into three basic translation
types, which we also call Type 0, Type 1 and Type 2.

   (i) enumerate all normal subgroups H of P ;
   (ii) for each H, enumerate the conjugacy classes of subgroups Si ⊂ SO(3) abstractly isomorphic to P/H;

   (iv) lift this isomorphism to a homomorphism ρP : P → SO(3);
   (v) merge equivalent homomorphisms.
   Combining the resulting ρP with the grading χ gives all Type 0 SSGs with parent space group G.

                                    4.   Continuous families and incommensurate orders


   The number of inequivalent Type 1 homomorphisms may be infinite because k varies continuously.
In some cases the allowed k form a line or a surface, which gives a continuous family of Type 1 SSGs.
Our method preserves k as a parameter, so that such a family is labelled by a single family name and
distinguished by the value of k. If k ∈ Q3 the magnetic order is commensurable, whereas if k ∈ R3 \Q3 it
is incommensurable. Only Type 1 SSGs can describe incommensurable orders, and a parent group G can
support them only if it possesses high-symmetry lines or surfaces on which ηp Rp k = k for some η.

                                                         5.   Algorithm


  For a given parent space group G, all Type 2 SSGs are enumerated as follows:
  (i) determine all nonzero t0 ∈ T/2T invariant under P modulo 2L (see the selection rule above); for
each allowed t0 , construct ρT from the annihilator triple {ki };
  (ii) for each ρT , determine σ : P → S3 from Rp ki = kσp (i) and construct the corresponding matrices
S(p);
  (iii) solve Eq. (38) under the constraint (37) to obtain all allowed θis , construct D(s(p)), and hence
ρ(g) = ρT (t)D(s(p))S(p);
  (iv) merge equivalent homomorphisms ρ;
  (v) combine all inequivalent ρ with the grading χ to obtain all Type 2 SSGs with parent space group G.


                                               G.     Equivalence relations


  Three kinds of equivalence must be distinguished.
  (1) SO(3) conjugation. If ρ1 (g) = Oρ2 (g)O−1 for all g ∈ G with O ∈ SO(3), the two homomorphisms
are equivalent. This equivalence is built into the enumeration: In Type 1, we select the rotation axis as z
axis and merge cases with k and −k. In Type 2, we set V4 = {E, Rx (π), Ry (π), Rz (π)} and set cannonical
choice of ki .
                                                                                                       13

  (2) Automorphisms of G. Two homomorphisms ϱ1 , ϱ2 : G → O(3) are equivalent if ϱ1 = ϱ2 ◦ α for an
automorphism α of G. We follow the physical convention that α must preserve the chirality of G. This
equivalence is not built into the algorithm and must be imposed after enumeration; it identifies SSGs that
describe the same magnetic order in different settings.
  (3) Continuous families. For Type 1 SSGs, wave vectors that are continuously connected and share the
same η belong to the same family. Here we follow Song’s convention: the family carries one label, and k
enters as a parameter. This is also built into the algorithm.
  Thus only the second equivalence has to be imposed separately after the enumeration.


                III.   ENUMERATION OF COPLANAR AND COLLINEAR SSGS


  Coplanar and collinear SSGs are degenerate cases of noncoplanar SSGs. In both cases the magnetic
moments are invariant under a nontrivial spin-only group S, so that the SSG is an extension of S by a
space group, and the enumeration reduces to homomorphisms into O(2) or O(1).


                                          A.   Coplanar SSGs


  When the moments are confined to a plane, which we take as the xy plane, the spin-only subgroup is

                              S = {E, PRz (π)},      P = diag(−1, −1, −1),                           (39)

               IV.    HIERARCHICAL CLASSIFICATION OF SPIN SPACE GROUPS


 Our enumeration algorithm provides a natural hierachical classification framework of SSGs. The homo-
morphism ϱ : G → O(n) leads to the the following homomorphism of group extension:

                                1            T              G               P            1
                                               ϱT             ϱ               ϱP

                                1         ϱT (T)          ϱ(G)            ϱP (P )        1

where ϱ(G) ⊂ NO(n) (ϱT (T)), ϱP (P ) = ϱ(G)/ϱT (T).
   We can define the following hierarchy of SSGs.
   First, we only consider the lattice group T, which defines a lattice L in R3 . Lattices are classified into
14 Bravais lattice types according to actions of normalizers of L in R3 ⋊ O(3). Now, only consider the
translation symmetry of a spin space group, which is determined by T and ϱ. T and ϱ determine a lattice
associated with spins at each site, which we call a spin lattice. The spatial symmetries of spin lattices
can still be classified into 14 Bravais lattice types. To further capture the spin symmetries of spin lattice,
we introuduce two new concepts: Translation holonomy type and spin Bravais lattice type. The former is
determined by ϱT (T), while the latter is determined by (T, ϱT ) and defined as:
   Translation holonomy type: Two spin lattices are in the same translation holonomy type if and only
if they have equivalent ϱT (T).
   Spin Bravais lattice type: Two spin lattices are in the same spin Bravais lattice type if and only if
they are in the same Bravais lattice type and have equivalent ϱT .
   Here we note, since collinear, coplanar and noncoplanar SSGs have different spin-only groups, their spin
lattices are always inequivalent and discussed seperately.
   According to this definition, there are infinite types of translation holonomy types. For convinence, we
collect infinite types into a family, and define the concetpt spin lattice types. Now we give a case by
case enumeration.
   Spin lattice types
   Collinear SSGs. There are two types of ϱT (T), trivial or Z2 . Thus we classifies collinear spin lattices into
two types 4 . Two collinear spin lattices are in the same spin lattice Bravais type if they have equivalent
χT . Collinear spin lattice Bravais types correpsond to magnetic Bravais lattice types.

   P2.1: ρT (T) = D1 = {1, σ}, whose normalizer is V4 = {E, −E, σ, −σ}. The action of V4 on ρT (T) is

               V.   17 SPIN-LATTICE TYPES AND THEIR MAGNETIC ORDERS


   In this section, we introduce the 17 spin-lattice types one by one and discuss the magnetic orders they
correpsond. Spin-lattice types are determined by the translation holonomy image ϱT (T) ∈ O(n), which
can be characterized by propogation wave vectors. We list propogation wave vectors for each type, and
list its magnetization profile. We also discuss what kinds of known magnetic orders belong to it and give
representative examples. Among all 17 spin-lattice types, 5 are not identified before.
   To write the magnetic profiles, we note the translation invariance requires

                               M (r + t) = ϱT (t)M (r) = χT (t)ρT (t)M (r),                              (65)

where χT (t) = eikχ ·t ∈ {±1} is the antiunitary translation grading governed by a time-reversal invariant
momentum (TRIM) kχ , and ρT (t) ∈ SO(3) is the proper spin rotation component. In the following, m0 (r)
or its Cartesian components denote cell-periodic amplitude profiles satisfying m0 (r + t) = m0 (r) for all
t ∈ L, and {êx , êy , êz } is a fixed orthonormal Cartesian basis in spin space.


                                       A.    Collinear Spin-Lattice Types


  In collinear spin lattices, all moments align along a single collinear axis, chosen here as êz . The proper
spin rotation is trivial (ρT (T) = {E}), and the translation holonomy is fully determined by the antiunitary
grading χT . χT (t) = eikχ ·t .


                                  1.    L.0.1: Translation-Invariant Collinear


  Definition: ϱT (t) = 1, with wave vector kχ = 0. ϱT (T) = {E}.
  Because lattice translations act trivially on all moments, the real-space magnetization profile is

                                                  M (r) = mz (r) êz .                                   (66)

The magnetic unit cell is identical to the underlying crystallographic primitive cell. This spin lattice type
encompasses conventional ferromagnets (such as bcc Fe and fcc Ni), ferrimagnets (such as Fe3 O4 and
yttrium iron garnet), and collinear altermagnets. Some antiferrimagnets also belongs to this type.


                                            2.   L.0.2: Staggered Collinear


   Definition: ϱT (t) = eikχ ·t , kχ ∈ TRIM. ϱT (T) = {E, P} ∼
                                                             = ZT2 .
   Translations by lattice vectors satisfying kχ · t = π (mod 2π) flip the spin orientation. The magnetic
structure takes the form

                                            M (r) = (−1)kχ ·r/π mz (r) êz .                             (67)

This type describes bipartite collinear antiferromagnets where opposite-spin sublattices are related by
real-space translations. Prominent examples include canonical type-II antiferromagnets such as NiO.

                                    B.     Coplanar Spin-Lattice Types


  In coplanar systems, the magnetic moments are confined to a single plane, chosen here as the xy spin
plane (mz (r) = 0). The spin-only group is S = {E, PRz (π)}. Because PRz (π) acts as the identity
within the xy plane, an independent antiunitary translation grading χT is redundant, and the translation
holonomy is represented by ϱT (T) = ρT (T) ⊂ O(2).



                                   1.    P.0: Translation-Invariant Coplanar


  Definition: ϱT (t) = I2 , with wave vector k = 0. ϱT (T) = {E}.
  Every lattice translation leaves the coplanar moment configuration unchanged:

                                M (r + t) = M (r) = mx (r) êx + my (r) êy .                            (68)

This type corresponds to coplanar magnets whose magnetic primitive cell coincides with the crystallo-
graphic primitive cell. Standard realizations include the triangular and kagome 120◦ chiral antiferromag-
nets, such as hexagonal Mn3 Sn, Mn3 Ge, and LuMnO3 .

  Definition: ϱT (t) = e−i(k·t)Jz , with wave vector k ∈ TRIM. ϱT (T) = {E, Rz (π)} ∼
                                                                                    = C2z .
   Within the spin plane, the π-rotation acts as Rz (π) = −I2 . The real-space magnetic configuration
alternates sign between translationally related sites:

                               M (r + t) = (−1)k·t/π [mx (r) êx + my (r) êy ] .                        (69)

While moments inside each unit cell remain noncollinear, the overall lattice forms a bipartite antifer-
romagnet under lattice translations. Typical representatives are iron-based superconductors displaying
noncollinear hedgehog spin-vortex-crystal (SVC) order, such as Ni- and Co-substituted CaKFe4 As4 .

                                  C.       Noncoplanar Spin-Lattice Types


  Noncoplanar spin lattices possess moments exploring all three dimensions of spin space. The spin-only
subgroup is trivial, and the antiunitary translation grading χT (t) = eikχ ·t acts independently from proper
spin rotations ρT .


                                1.    N.0.1: Translation-Invariant Noncoplanar


  Definition: ϱT (t) = I3 , with wave vector kχ = 0. ϱT (T) = {E}.

The full three-dimensional noncoplanar magnetic configuration resides entirely within the crystallographic
primitive cell. Prototypical materials are all-in-all-out (AIAO) tetrahedral orders on pyrochlore lattices,
such as the 5d pyrochlore iridates Eu2 Ir2 O7 and Nd2 Ir2 O7 .

                         B.     Connection to Bertaut’s representation analysis


  Bertaut’s representation analysis of magnetic structures is widely used in neutron diffraction refinements.
In this method, one fixes a propagation vector, forms the little group of the wave vector, and decomposes
the magnetic structure into basis vectors of the irreducible representations of the little group; the basis
vectors determine the allowed directions of the Fourier components.
  In our framework, if the spin-lattice type is choosen, the basis vectors of the magnetic structure are
determined, requiring no representation analysis. If we further consider point group symmetries in SSGs,
magnetic momenta at different sites can be further constrained. Usually, SSG can reduce more free
parameters in magnetic structure refinement than MSGs.

                VIII.   RELATION TO OTHER ENUMERATION FRAMEWORKS


  There is a direct connection between our framework and Song’s. In Song’s framework, homomorphisms
ϱ : G → O(3) are enumerated by combining irreducible representations of G. The restriction of ϱ to
T is ϱT , which decomposes into irreducible representations of T labelled by momenta k. The names of
the SSGs in Song’s framework contain these momenta explicitly, which provides some information of the
spin-lattice type of a given SSG in Song’s framework.

  Since only spin-lattice type P.2.2, N.2.1, N.2.3 can host a noncommuting Brillouin zone, this explains
why, in Song’s framework, a noncommuting Brillouin zone can appear only in types III and VII for coplanar
SSGs and in types IX, XIII and XVI for noncoplanar SSGs.


                                  IX.   SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK


  We have established a physically transparent classification framework for spin space groups and crystal
magnetic orders, grounded in the translation holonomy, i.e., the image of the lattice translations under the
spin homomorphism. By treating translations and point-group operations hierarchically, in the spirit of
the Zassenhaus classification of space groups, SSGs are organized into three basic translation types and 17
universal spin-lattice types. Each type displays a distinct signature in momentum and polarization space,
so that abstract group theory is connected directly to polarized neutron diffraction.
  The framework has several practical merits. It answers which magnetic orders are possible or impossible
for a given parent space group, it assigns an unambiguous diffraction signature to every spin-lattice type,

to a finer classification of magnetic orders, in the same way that the point-group levels refine the Bravais
